# Harnessing Image Question Dependence for Better VLM Test-time Reinforcement Learning 

## Xinrui He, Ting-Wei Li, Junting Wang, Mengting Ai, Xinyu He, Hanghang Tong, Jingrui He University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign Champaign, IL, USA 

# Abstract 

Test-time reinforcement learning can adapt vision-language mod-els (VLMs) to unlabeled target data, but its effectiveness is fundamentally limited by the reliability of self-generated learn-ing signals. To assess the reliability of consensus-based learn-ing signals, we analyze VLM test-time reinforcement learning across diverse VQA datasets and model sizes, revealing two limitations. First, gains from consensus-based test-time training largely come from answer normalization rather than content correction. Second, many initial VLM responses are incorrect due to the model’s limited ability to jointly use the image and the question; consensus rewards derived from these outputs may preserve the resulting grounding errors rather than correct them. Motivated by these, we propose TTIQ, a test-time reinforcement learning framework that harnesses image-question dependence for better vlm adaptation. TTIQ teacher-forces each sampled response under the original image-question pair and its image-and question-ablated variants, using the resulting token-level likelihood changes to estimate dependence on each input. It combines image and question dependence with calibrated con-fidence to construct a response-level reward that favors jointly grounded responses, and uses the token-level signals to assign greater positive policy credit to tokens supported by both inputs. This design favors responses that are jointly grounded in the image and the question and sufficiently confident, rather than merely popular. Experiments across eight VQA datasets and multiple VLM sizes show that TTIQ achieves the best average performance at every model scale. It further generalizes across VLM families, while models trained on one dataset improve performance on unseen datasets without further training.

To characterize these gaps, we first conduct an in-depth anal-ysis across diverse datasets and VLM scales, summarized here and detailed in Section 3.1. Our analysis reveals two recurring limitations. First, the gains of consensus-based VLM test-time reinforcement learning largely come from answer normal-ization rather than content correction. As shown in Figure 1, across model scales, most apparent corrections are form-only: exact-match accuracy improves even as ground-truth answer con-tainment slightly decreases; Second, most VLM errors exhibit insufficient joint use of the image and the question, which consensus rewards may then inherit. As shown in Figure 2, we examine the sampled responses used to construct consensus rewards and find that most incorrect responses lack dependence on at least one input. When such errors recur across rollouts, consensus may treat them as reliable signals and reinforce rather than correct them. To address these challenges, we propose TTIQ, a test-time reinforcement learning method for VLMs that rewards responses according to their joint dependence on the image and the ques-tion, rather than agreement among sampled outputs. As illus-trated in Figure 3, TTIQ teacher-forces each sampled response under the original input and its image- and question-ablated variants, and uses the resulting likelihood changes to estimate dependence on each input. At the response level , it combines these dependence signals with calibrated confidence to reward grounded and reliable responses. At the token level , it assigns greater positive policy credit to tokens supported by both inputs. This design shifts the learning signal from output popularity toward responses that are jointly grounded in the image and the question and sufficiently confident. Our contributions are: • We analyze consensus-based VLM test-time reinforcement learning across diverse VQA datasets and models, show-ing that its gains largely come from answer normalization rather than content correction, while many initial errors reflect limited joint use of the image and the question and can be preserved by consensus-based rewards. • We introduce TTIQ, the first test-time reinforcement learn-ing method for generative VLMs to explicitly incorporate visual grounding into its training signal. It estimates im-age and question dependence through image and question ablations, and uses these signals for response-level reward construction and token-level credit assignment. • Extensive experiments show TTIQ achieves the best aver-age performance at every evaluated model scale. It gen-eralizes across VLM families, and models trained on one dataset consistently improve performance on unseen datasets without further training.

Consensus is constrained by VLM’s initial failures. We next examine a more fundamental limitation of output-consensus supervision. Because consensus rewards are derived from the model’s own sampled responses, their effectiveness is limited by the quality of those responses. When the initial responses are incorrect, agreement among them may preserve the error rather than improve the model’s underlying capability. This concern is particularly relevant for VLM errors, as prior work shows that when generating responses, models tends to rely heavily on language priors while underusing visual evidence [Asadi et al., 2026, Lee et al., 2025]. We conduct a diagnostic with InternVL2.5-4B using 200 examples from each of eight datasets and four rollouts per example, yielding 6,400 responses. For each response, we keep its tokens fixed and compare their likelihoods under the original input, a zero-image input, and a question-masked input. A sustained likelihood drop after ablat-ing an input indicates dependence on that input. As shown in Figure 2, 69.0% of incorrect responses lack dependence on at least one input. This finding identifies limited joint use of the image and the question as one of the major sources of VLM errors.

## 3.3 Joint-Token Credit Assignment 

The above response-level reward determines whether a sampled response should be reinforced or suppressed, but it does not dis-tinguish the contributions of individual tokens. A positively re-warded response may contain grounded answer tokens together with generic templates, formatting phrases, or unsupported ex-planations. Applying the same positive advantage to every token would reinforce all of them equally. We therefore redistribute positive credit according to token level image–question depen-dence. For negatively rewarded responses, however, we retain the full response-level penalty for every token so that no part of a disfavored response is exempted from suppression. ## 4.4 Ablation Study 

We conduct component ablations on InternVL2.5-4B over four representative tasks. All variants use the same training split and common hyperparameters and use eight updates. First, we vary the reward construction, then compare full-response credit with joint-token credit. Finally, we study the contribution of confidence. Table 4 yields three main observations. First, image and ques-tion dependence both contribute to robust reward construction. Removing either signal reduces average accuracy. Although the image-only reward remains competitive overall, the question-only reward degrades substantially on GQA-Relation, showing that single-input signals are not consistently reliable across tasks. Second, the aggregation design is important. Minimum fusion outperforms mean fusion, while incorporating sustained depen-dence improves over using only the overall dependence level by 8.08 points. These results support requiring every reward component to be strong and measuring whether dependence per-sists across the response. Third, joint-token credit and calibrated confidence provide complementary improvements. Token-level credit better reinforces tokens supported by both inputs, while confidence further filters insufficiently reliable responses. Com-bining all components gives the strongest overall performance.

# 5 Related Work 

LLM and VLM test-time reinforcement learning. Test-time training Sun et al. [2020, 2025] adapts pretrained models us-ing unlabeled target inputs and has recently been extended to large language-model (LLM) reasoning through reinforcement learning. Existing work in this direction has primarily focused on LLMs. TTRL uses majority-vote responses from repeated sampling as pseudo-labels Zuo et al. [2026]; subsequent work improves this consensus signal using step-wise confidence Wang et al. [2026], or analyzes how majority voting can suppress ini-tially rare correct responses Lin et al. [2026]. In comparison, test-time reinforcement learning for generative VLMs remains less explored. TTRV applies this paradigm to VLMs using response-frequency rewards and the empirical answer distribution Singh et al. [2026]. Existing approaches therefore largely derive super-vision from consistency within the model’s own outputs He et al. [2025], leaving it unclear whether the resulting improvements reflect stronger multimodal understanding or merely greater output consistency. 

Grounding in VLMs. Despite their strong performance, VLMs often produce responses without faithfully using the pro-vided visual evidence. Recent analyses reveal different forms of this failure Tong et al. [2024], Li et al. [2024]. Mirage shows that frontier VLMs can retain substantial benchmark performance and generate detailed visual reasoning even without image ac-cess Asadi et al. [2026]. VLind-Bench further isolates language-prior-induced blindness through counterfactual tests Lee et al. [2025]. Motivated by these grounding failures, a complemen-tary line of work leverages visual grounding signals to improve model predictions at inference time. VCD contrasts predictions under original and distorted images to reduce reliance on lan-guage priors Leng et al. [2024], while entropy-based methods use model uncertainty to identify or retrieve relevant visual ev-idence Gröpl et al. [2026]. Grounding signals have also been incorporated into VLM training through fine-grained feedback and reward modeling Yu et al. [2024], Yan et al. [2024]. Given both the prevalence of grounding failures and the demonstrated effectiveness of grounding signals in inference and training, their use as supervision for unlabeled test-time fine-tuning represents a promising yet comparatively underexplored direction. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We studied test-time reinforcement learning for VLMs and iden-tified two limitations of output-consensus supervision. Moti-7vated by these findings, we proposed TTIQ, which estimates image and question dependence through controlled input abla-tions and uses the resulting signals for response-level reward construction and token-level credit assignment. Experiments across diverse VQA tasks and VLM scales show that TTIQ achieves the best average performance at every evaluated scale. It also generalizes across VLM families and improves perfor-mance on unseen datasets without further training. These results suggest that image-question dependence learning signals provide a promising alternative to output consensus for VLM test-time reinforcement learning.

# B Training-Set Size and Efficiency 

## B.1 Impact of Training-Set Size 

We evaluate TTIQ and TTRV using nested sets of 20, 50, and 100 unlabeled examples on four representative tasks with InternVL2.5-4B. All settings use the same held-out split ob-tained by excluding the full 100-example training pool. We increase the number of updates proportionally with the training-set size, keeping the average number of exposures per example approximately constant. Table 6 and Figure 5 show distinct scaling behavior. TTIQ achieves strong macro result with only 20 examples and remains broadly stable as more examples are added. In contrast, TTRV improves more with training-set size, but its result with 100 examples still remains below that of TTIQ with 20 examples. TTIQ also wins on most individual task–size combinations. This contrast reflects a fundamental difference in their supervision signals. TTRV relies on response agreement as an indirect proxy for correctness, whose useful effects may accumulate over more training examples. In contrast, TTIQ uses controlled input ab-lations to expose, within each example, whether a response is grounded in both the image and the question. It therefore di-rectly corrects how the model uses multimodal evidence rather than relying on a useful training signal to emerge across many examples. Once this effective signal is available, increasing the training set mainly broadens example coverage, resulting in limited and task-dependent gains beyond 20 examples.

In the bottom four examples, the incorrect response has weak image dependence and relies more heavily on textual cues or language priors, resulting in an answer that is not sufficiently 12 supported by the image. These examples show that strong de-pendence on either input alone is insufficient. TTIQ therefore rewards responses only when they depend strongly on both the image and the question.
